\begin{abstract}
After a disruption, a job shop must be rescheduled within a wall-clock budget. We study, under one controlled pipeline, four questions about what actually drives anytime quality in this setting: whether a feasible incumbent and a single selective reoptimization stage help at all beyond a hinted but otherwise unrestricted solver; whether repeating that restricted stage several times helps further; whether \emph{how} each round's neighbourhood is chosen (by the incumbent's critical region, by a random subset, by a time window, or by relaxing random precedences) matters once repetition itself is accounted for; and whether any of this survives the installed solver's own multi-worker scheduling search. On a frozen, pre-registered confirmatory study (40 held-out streams, five solver seeds, nine settings, 60{,}720 validated runs, independently re-verified from raw trajectories with a second, code-disjoint pipeline), repeating the restricted stage with a recomputed, spatially-coherent neighbourhood reduces the primal integral by \UOneBTwocritVsBZeroiaipRel\% against a single-round incumbent-aware pipeline (Holm $p=\UOneBTwocritVsBZeroiaipHolm$) and by \UOneBTwocritVsBFivenativeOnewRel\% against the solver's own single-thread scheduling search (confirmed active from solver logs). Repetition with an \emph{un}structured neighbourhood adds almost nothing on its own ($\UOneBOnerandomVsBZeroiaipRel$\%, not significant). Which structured neighbourhood is used matters far less than whether one is used: a size-matched time-window neighbourhood is not significantly different from the incumbent's critical region overall; critical-region targeting is ahead when an end-to-end budget lets it complete more rounds and behind when round counts are matched, so the sign depends on which resource is matched and no superiority is established. With four CP-SAT workers, the advantage over native search is no longer statistically established. A frozen, held-out cross-solver study with ten CP and MILP engines (three commercial ones only under free-edition size limits) shows that retaining an early validated incumbent outside the solver lowers the primal integral for every engine, whereas the value of repeated restricted rounds depends on engine and instance size: consistently positive in direction for all uncapped engines at paper scale, mixed on small instances. We report this as a bounded, empirical finding about an engineering pipeline, not as evidence for a new search algorithm.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Rescheduling under disruption and a strict time budget is standard in dynamic job-shop scheduling \citep{vieira2003,ouelhadj2009}. A constraint-programming pipeline for this task typically (i) keeps a validated incumbent built from the previous schedule or from dispatching rules, (ii) selectively re-optimizes a restricted neighbourhood of the operations a disruption affects, and (iii) spends the remaining budget on the unrestricted problem, hinted with the current incumbent. Selective CP-SAT repair of this kind is used with a learned keep/release policy by recent work \citep{linyang2026}, and the choice of rescheduling scope has itself been studied directly \citep{sun2026}.

None of the individual ingredients here is new: incumbent portfolios, selective repair, critical-path neighbourhoods and repeated large neighbourhood search (LNS) are all established \citep{balas1969,vanlaarhoven1992,nowicki1996,shaw1998,godard2005,gomes2001}. What this paper investigates as a controlled empirical and algorithm-engineering question is the interaction between six design choices a practitioner must make deploying such a pipeline, summarized as RQ1--RQ6 below and answered in Figures~\ref{fig:traj}--\ref{fig:heatmap}. A prior stage of this project additionally built a sound lower-bound certificate meant to decide these questions mathematically; it changed almost no decisions in practice, because the installed solver already disposes of non-improving neighbourhoods in milliseconds on its own (the certificate section below). We treat that as a completed negative result and ask the remaining, purely empirical question directly.

\paragraph{Research questions.} RQ1: does retaining a feasible incumbent and running one selective stage help beyond fresh, unrestricted solving? RQ2: does repeating the restricted stage help beyond a single selective stage? RQ3: does \emph{how} the repeated neighbourhood is chosen matter, and does the answer depend on disruption type? RQ4: how does the value of this entire external pipeline change when CP-SAT is given more workers of its own? RQ5: is any observed improvement explained by neighbourhood content, or by the number of completed rounds? RQ6: do the answers generalize across budgets, severities, and published benchmark instances? RQ7: which of these answers transfer to other CP and MILP engines (RQ-S1--S6 of the cross-solver section)?

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) An integrated, independently-verified pipeline and size-matched external analogues of three of CP-SAT's own internal scheduling-LNS generators, confirmed active or inactive from saved solver logs rather than assumed (the neighbourhood-targeting section). (2) A frozen, pre-registered confirmatory study answering RQ1--RQ6 with 60{,}720 independently-reproduced runs (the results section). (3) Identification of a round-throughput confound in neighbourhood-selection comparisons: an apparent arrival-specific advantage of critical-region targeting (RQ3) is not robust to matching round counts, and the comparison's sign depends on whether rounds or restricted-solver time is matched (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}) --- reported because it narrows a conclusion we would otherwise have drawn too generously. (4) Explicit negative findings alongside the positive ones: repetition alone is not sufficient (RQ2 partially negative), neighbourhood \emph{choice} among structured alternatives is not established to matter beyond time-locality (RQ3 negative), and the advantage over native search is not established at four workers (RQ4). (5) A frozen, held-out cross-solver evaluation of the same strategies over ten CP and MILP engines and three formulations (the cross-solver section), separating what transfers (external incumbent retention) from what is engine- and size-dependent (restricted rounds).

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Selective rescheduling.} Affected-operations rescheduling re-optimizes only the operations a disruption affects \citep{abumaizar1997,subramaniam2003}. \citet{sun2026} compare four hierarchical optimization scopes for dynamic flexible job shops and report that scope definition, not search algorithm, is decisive under strict time limits. SLR2 \citep{linyang2026} encodes the incumbent and the disturbance in a heterogeneous graph and uses a graph neural network to learn a kept/released split, repairing the released neighbourhood with a reduced CP-SAT model. We attempted legitimate access to both full texts (publisher page, DOI redirect, author/aggregator search) and were able to access only their abstracts (ScienceDirect returns an access-controlled redirect to automated requests, and no preprint or author-hosted copy exists); our comparison is accordingly limited to what their abstracts state, flagged here rather than inferred further. Our release rule is structural rather than learned, and we evaluate anytime primal integral under one end-to-end deadline rather than final solution quality.

\paragraph{Critical-path search and LNS.} In the disjunctive graph of a complete selection, the makespan equals the longest path \citep{balas1969}; neighbourhoods built from critical arcs or critical blocks are classical in job-shop local search \citep{vanlaarhoven1992,nowicki1996,balas1998}. Large neighbourhood search with constraint programming \citep{shaw1998,godard2005,pacino2011} descends from the shuffle heuristic for job shops \citep{applegate1991}. We do not claim critical-path targeting itself is new, nor repeated LNS; the neighbourhood-targeting section and Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} ask whether, in this pipeline, critical targeting adds value over the alternatives CP-SAT already implements internally.

\paragraph{CP-SAT's internal scheduling search.} Inspecting the OR-Tools v9.15 source (\texttt{cp\_model\_lns.h/.cc}, \texttt{cp\_model\_solver.cc}) shows CP-SAT ships four scheduling-specific LNS generators (random-interval, time-window, resource-window, random-precedence), each restricting a no-overlap/cumulative model to a relaxed subset of intervals while keeping the incumbent's precedences among the rest --- the same restriction structure our restricted model uses. We verified, from saved solver-log transcripts on the actual confirmatory instances, that these generators are inactive with a single default-configured worker, and active both with \texttt{interleave\_search} on one worker and with CP-SAT's default multi-worker portfolio; per-run subsolver filtering is exposed to Python callers (\texttt{fil\-ter\_\-sub\-solvers}/\texttt{ex\-tra\_\-sub\-solvers}/\texttt{ig\-nore\_\-sub\-solvers}), though we did not use it, running the solver's ordinary default and interleaved configurations unmodified instead (Table~\ref{tab:methods}).

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:setting}

A \emph{stream} is a sequence of job-shop instances $I_0,\dots,I_T$, with $I_t$ obtained from $I_{t-1}$ by an arrival, a cancellation, a duration change or a machine outage. Operations have integer durations, job precedence, machine no-overlap (including outage intervals) and optional release dates and commitments. Each instance is solved within a wall-clock budget $B$ covering construction, model building, solving and validation.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Symbol & Meaning \\
\midrule
$I_t$ & instance at step $t$ of a stream \\
$B$ & wall-clock budget per instance \\
$z(\tau)$, $z^*$ & best makespan by time $\tau$; best found by any method \\
$g(\tau)$ & relative gap, Eq.~\ref{eq:gap} \\
$\mathrm{PI}$ & anytime primal integral, Eq.~\ref{eq:pi} \\
$N$ & round's restricted neighbourhood (released ops.) \\
$U$ & incumbent makespan ($C_{\max}\le U{-}1$ in $N$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Notation used throughout.}
\label{tab:notation}
\end{table}

\begin{equation}
g(\tau) = \begin{cases} 1 & \tau < t_{\mathrm{first}} \\ (z(\tau)-z^*)/z^* & \tau \ge t_{\mathrm{first}} \end{cases}
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PI} = \frac{1}{B}\int_0^B g(\tau)\,d\tau
\label{eq:pi}
\end{equation}
with $z^*$ shared per instance across all compared methods \citep{berthold2013}.

\section{Incumbent and Restricted-Reoptimization Framework}
\label{sec:method}

The base pipeline \base{} (reused unchanged from a prior stage of this project) (i) builds a portfolio of validated incumbents --- repairs of the previous schedule plus dispatching rules --- retaining each improving member as soon as it is built; (ii) restricts CP-SAT to a 25\%-of-operations breadth-first neighbourhood grown from the disrupted operations, with $C_{\max}\le U-1$, until $0.6B$ elapsed or a proof of optimality/infeasibility of the restricted problem; (iii) continues with unrestricted, hinted CP-SAT until $B$. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} shows this pipeline and which stages each compared method includes. Our integration (\crit{} and its matched controls, Algorithm~\ref{alg:round}) repeats step (ii) while the local window remains and the previous round improved the incumbent, recomputing the targeting-relevant structure each round rather than reusing a stale one.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\scalebox{0.92}{\input{figures/figure1_framework.tex}}
\caption{The unified pipeline and stage presence by method; $U(t)=\min\{C_{\max}(S): S$ a validated incumbent by time $t\}$.}
\label{fig:framework}
\end{figure*}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One round of repeated restricted reoptimization}
\label{alg:round}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE incumbent $S$, disrupted operations $D$, targeting rule $\tau$, local deadline $T$
\STATE recompute $S$'s longest binding chains (calendar-aware: outages, releases, commitments)
\STATE build $N$ from $\tau$ (critical chain / random / time window / random precedence), seeded at $D$, $|N|$ matched across rules
\STATE solve $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{imp}}(S,N)$: the base neighbourhood $\mathcal{P}(S,N)$ (full model + $S$'s machine order outside $N$) plus $C_{\max}\le U{-}1$; hint $S$
\IF{a validated $S'$ is returned}
  \STATE $S \gets S'$; repeat while time $<T$ remains
\ELSE
  \STATE INFEASIBLE: no strictly better schedule in $\mathcal{P}(S,N)$; UNKNOWN/time-out: no conclusion. Stop (M1) \textbf{or} continue to the fixed round count (M2)
\ENDIF
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

One monotonic deadline covers the portfolio, every round, and the final unrestricted stage. The incumbent $S$ satisfies every kept arc, so $S\in\mathcal{P}(S,N)$; but it violates the cutoff, so $S\notin\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{imp}}(S,N)$, and the hint is a search hint, not a feasible start. The incumbent is therefore retained \emph{outside} the solver and replaced only by a validated strictly better schedule, which makes the retained objective non-increasing. An INFEASIBLE round is a proof that $\mathcal{P}(S,N)$ contains nothing better (\RestrStatusInfeasible{} of the \RestrStatusTotal{} restricted solves in the confirmatory campaign); an UNKNOWN round (\RestrStatusUnknown) or a capped FEASIBLE round (\RestrStatusFeasible{}, improved without an optimality proof) proves nothing about the rest of $\mathcal{P}(S,N)$. All outcomes are recorded.

\subsection{Neighbourhood-targeting strategies (RQ3)}
\label{sec:lns}

Every control in Table~\ref{tab:methods} shares the identical initial incumbent, round policy, local window, global continuation and worker count as \crit{}, differing only in how each round's neighbourhood is chosen, so that any PI difference is attributable to neighbourhood \emph{content} rather than to a resource asymmetry. \tw{} and \precn{} are external, size-matched analogues of CP-SAT's own \texttt{Scheduling\-Time\-Window\-Neighborhood\-Generator} and \texttt{Random\-Precedence\-Scheduling\-Neighborhood\-Generator}.

Two protocols measure different things. \textbf{M1} (end-to-end) runs each method until the local window closes or a round fails to improve: it measures what a deployed pipeline obtains, and round counts differ between methods. \textbf{M2} fixes 6 rounds of at most 0.4\,s each, regardless of success: it equalizes the \emph{number} of rounds, but not the restricted-solver time a round consumes, the difficulty of the restricted subproblem, presolve effort, or the time left for the global continuation (all measured below). Neither protocol isolates neighbourhood content alone.

For RQ4, \native{} is \ph{} run with \texttt{interleave\_search} on one worker, and \ph{} at four workers is CP-SAT's default multi-worker portfolio; both were verified from saved logs to activate all four internal scheduling LNS generators (\texttt{re\-sults/\-fi\-nal\_i\-caps/\-sol\-ver\_logs/}). We compare native search only at matched worker count, never across worker counts; at four workers (U3), the comparison uses $4\times$ the CPU-seconds of every one-worker setting by design, since U3 tests whether the external pipeline's advantage survives the solver's own parallel search, not whether CPU-seconds are held equal (a resource-normalized, equal-core-second variant was not run; see Discussion and Limitations).

\subsection{Correctness and the certificate result}
\label{sec:certificate}

The restriction machinery (kept-arc construction, the restricted model builder, the critical-chain computation) is reused unchanged from a prior stage, verified against two independent oracles (full enumeration and an independently-built CP-SAT model) on \OracleEnumCases\ and \OracleCpsatCases\ randomized cases with zero bound violations; for this study we additionally verified, on 250 randomized instances across all ten neighbourhood strategies with outages, release dates and commitments, that every restricted optimum lies between the full-problem optimum and the incumbent's value. Failed or non-improving rounds are recorded, resumption reproduces byte-identical output, and all 487 tests pass. A sound lower-bound \emph{certificate} for a restricted neighbourhood, verified against 5{,}500 independent oracle cases with zero violations, was decisive for only 13 of 200 restricted neighbourhoods tested; CP-SAT itself already proves the same non-improvement in a median of 21\,ms, and certificate-gated skipping, releasing, or budget allocation produced no measurable change in primal integral. We report this as a completed negative result.

\section{Matched-Round and Neighbourhood Analysis}
\label{sec:timewindow}

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} answers RQ3 from already-archived U1/U2/U6/U7 runs; no new experiments were run, and every quantity in the figure is recomputed from raw run records. Under M1 (Panel A, confirmatory), \crit{} is significantly better than \tw{} only in the arrival-only setting (U6), not overall (U1) and not after outages (U7). Under M2 (Panel B) the overall sign reverses: \crit{} is \UTwoMTwocritVsMTwotimewinRel\% \emph{worse} than \tw{} (CI \UTwoMTwocritVsMTwotimewinCIRel\%, Holm $p=\UTwoMTwocritVsMTwotimewinHolm$). In an exploratory breakdown of the same M2 runs (uncorrected, outside the pre-registered family) the point estimate favours \tw{} in all four disruption types, with the CI excluding zero for arrival ($+\MechTwoArrivalRel$\%, CI \MechTwoArrivalCI\%), cancellation and duration change, but not for outage ($+\MechTwoOutageRel$\%, CI \MechTwoOutageCI\%).

Panels C and D show what each protocol holds fixed. Under M1 the two rules spend almost the same restricted-solver time per instance (\RestrSMOneCrit{} vs.\ \RestrSMOneTw\,s), but \crit{} completes more rounds (\RoundsMOneCrit{} vs.\ \RoundsMOneTw), because its rounds improve more often (\ImprRateMOneCrit\% vs.\ \ImprRateMOneTw\%) and M1 continues only after an improvement. Under M2 the round counts are equal (\RoundsMTwoCrit{} vs.\ \RoundsMTwoTw), but \tw{} then receives \RestrMTwoTwVsCritPct\% more restricted-solver time (\RestrSMTwoTw{} vs.\ \RestrSMTwoCrit\,s), at the expense of the global stage. The sign of the comparison therefore depends on which resource is matched, and neither protocol isolates neighbourhood content. \textbf{We do not claim that critical-region targeting is better suited to any disruption type}; the evidence establishes only that its advantage under M1 is not robust to matching round counts. This supersedes our own earlier reading of the U6 result.

Neighbourhood \emph{size} is matched almost exactly (\MTwoRoundCritN{} vs.\ \MTwoRoundTwN{} released operations per round under M2), but subproblem \emph{difficulty} is not: time-window rounds need \MTwoRoundTwVsCritPct\% more solver time per round (\MTwoRoundTwSolverS{} vs.\ \MTwoRoundCritSolverS\,s) and more often hit the 0.4\,s cap without a proof (\MTwoRoundTwFeas{} vs.\ \MTwoRoundCritFeas{} capped improving rounds; \MTwoRoundTwUnknown{} vs.\ \MTwoRoundCritUnknown{} rounds with neither improvement nor proof), while critical-region rounds more often end in a proof that the neighbourhood contains nothing better (\MTwoRoundCritInfeas{} vs.\ \MTwoRoundTwInfeas). Equal neighbourhood size does not imply equal search effort.

The study above uses one solver. To ask which findings belong to the pipeline rather than to CP-SAT, we ported the instances, objective and stages to every engine we could legitimately run, and froze a held-out plan (tiers, streams, methods, budgets, seeds, Holm families, and each engine's ``best'' configuration chosen on development streams only) before any held-out run. \section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Per our pre-registered classification, this is a \textbf{CONDITIONAL GO}: C1, C2, C4 and C5 hold reproducibly; C3 is not established as superior (and, after reanalysis, not even conditionally so after arrivals); C6 fails under the pre-registered rule. Six CP-SAT findings follow, each with a practical consequence for a practitioner deploying this pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Repeated restricted optimization helps.} Recomputing and re-solving a restricted neighbourhood several times, rather than once, substantially improves anytime quality over \base{} (Figure~\ref{fig:forest}). \emph{Consequence:} a single-round selective-repair pipeline is leaving quality on the table if the local time window has not yet closed.
\item \textbf{Structured targeting beats some random alternatives.} \crit{} beats \rnd{} and \precn{}, which are not themselves reliably better than \base{} (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Figure~\ref{fig:forest}). \emph{Consequence:} the neighbourhood must be spatially coherent, not merely repeated; an arbitrary or precedence-only relaxation is not a substitute.
\item \textbf{Critical-region targeting is not established as superior to time-window targeting.} Its only significant M1 advantage (arrival-only) is not robust to matching round counts, under which the point estimates favour time-window (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}A--B). \emph{Consequence:} a practitioner may use the simpler time-window rule without a demonstrated quality cost.
\item \textbf{Completed-round throughput materially matters.} The sign changes with the matched resource (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}C--D). \emph{Consequence:} compare targeting rules with both round counts and restricted-solver time reported.
\item \textbf{With more workers, the external pipeline's advantage is no longer established.} Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}'s \textsc{4 workers} column: the difference to \ph{} narrows from \UOneBTwocritVsBSixporthintRel\% at one worker to \UThreeBTwocritVsBSixporthintRel\% at four, not significant under the sign test although its CI excludes zero, at $4\times$ the CPU-seconds. \emph{Consequence:} this pipeline's value is largest precisely where CP-SAT's own parallel search is most constrained (single-worker deployments).
\item \textbf{Strategy value depends on budget and disruption type.} Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} shows the advantage is weakest at the shortest budget (U4) and strongest on published benchmark bases (U9); it is not a uniform multiplier. \emph{Consequence:} re-measure the payoff for a deployment's budget and workload.
\end{enumerate}

We do \emph{not} claim critical-region search is a superior targeting mechanism; the defensible claim is narrower (finding 3 above). Instances are synthetic full job shops from one generator; development and held-out streams share that generator. $z^*$ is the best value found in each setting, not a proven optimum. Only makespan is optimized; stability is not controlled. The C3 equivalence classification depends on a pre-specified $\pm10\%$ margin and a development-derived power estimate; non-significance under that margin is absence of demonstrated superiority, not proof of equivalence. CP-SAT's internal LNS generator selection is solver-version-specific; a different OR-Tools release could show different four-worker behaviour. U3 matches wall-clock, not core-seconds; an equal-CPU variant and a scheduling-LNS-only native baseline are future work. The full texts of \citet{sun2026} and \citet{linyang2026} remain inaccessible to us despite a renewed, documented attempt through legitimate routes. In the cross-solver study, commercial engines ran only under free-edition size caps, so the paper-scale tiers exclude CP Optimizer, Gurobi and CPLEX; those tiers have five streams, too few for the frozen sign test to reach significance; restricted rounds are hinted on only two engines, and CBC and GLPK trajectories are observable only on return; each technology uses one formulation, so engine differences are differences between engine--model pairs; and the host was shared with an unrelated workload (median CPU/wall \MSCpuWall{} for one-worker runs).

\section{Conclusion}

Across a frozen, independently-reproduced confirmatory study, retaining a feasible incumbent and repeating a restricted CP-SAT stage with a recomputed, spatially-coherent neighbourhood reliably improves an anytime rescheduling pipeline over a single restricted stage, over unstructured repetition, and over the installed solver's single-thread scheduling search. Whether that coherent region should be the schedule's critical chain specifically, rather than a comparably simple time window, is not established by this evidence in any disruption type we tested once round counts are matched; and the advantage over native search is no longer statistically established once the solver uses four workers of its own. Across ten CP and MILP engines, the early validated incumbent is the portable part of the pipeline; restricted rounds help every uncapped engine in direction at paper scale, but their value on small instances is engine-specific, and no engine-independent superiority of the method follows from this evidence. We report positive and negative findings together, including the reanalysis that narrowed our own initial reading.
